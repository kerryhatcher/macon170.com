# MTSS (Multi-Tiered System of Supports): Research for Cub Scout Pack Community Partnership

## Overview: What Is MTSS?

**Multi-Tiered System of Supports (MTSS)** is a comprehensive, data-driven framework that helps schools identify and support students' academic, behavioral, and social-emotional needs through a tiered system of interventions. MTSS integrates two earlier models—Response to Intervention (RTI), which focused on academic support, and Positive Behavioral Interventions and Supports (PBIS), which emphasized behavioral support—into one cohesive whole-child system.

The framework operates on the principle that all students deserve high-quality instruction and support, with intensity matched to need. Rather than the traditional "wait to fail" approach, MTSS emphasizes prevention and early intervention, identifying struggling students before challenges become severe.

## The Three-Tier Model

### Tier 1: Universal Supports (75–90% of students)

**What it is:** Core instruction and behavioral expectations delivered to all students in high-quality, evidence-based classroom teaching, schoolwide expectations, and proactive classroom management.

**Key characteristics:**
- High-quality core academic instruction aligned to standards
- Clear, explicitly taught schoolwide behavioral expectations (e.g., "Be Respectful," "Be Responsible," "Be Safe")
- Consistent positive reinforcement for meeting expectations
- Systematic classroom management procedures
- Visual aids, structured lessons, and organized systems
- Universal screening to identify students who need additional support

**When it works:** Most students succeed with Tier 1 supports alone when they receive consistent, quality instruction and positive behavioral support.

**Examples in an elementary school:**
- Daily explicit teaching of classroom expectations in all settings (classroom, hallway, cafeteria, playground)
- Whole-group morning meetings that build community and discuss the day's focus
- Token or point systems that recognize positive behavior across the school
- Clear, consistent consequences for rule violations
- Teacher-led classroom circles or discussions about respect and problem-solving
- Physical activity and structured recess with clear expectations
- Grade-level assemblies reinforcing behavioral themes

### Tier 2: Targeted Interventions (10–25% of students)

**What it is:** Small-group, standardized interventions designed for students who are not succeeding with Tier 1 supports alone. These interventions address specific academic, behavioral, or social-emotional needs and should be accessible within 72 hours of referral.

**Key characteristics:**
- Accessible and low-effort to implement
- Continuously available and aligned with Tier 1 expectations
- Group interventions (10 or more students in a group)
- Data-driven and frequently monitored (progress reviewed every 1–2 weeks)
- Based on identified function of behavior or skill gap
- Flexible and based on ongoing assessment
- Can be implemented by typical school staff
- Provide increased adult supervision and positive reinforcement

**Effectiveness:** Tier 2 interventions implemented with fidelity are estimated to have positive effects for 50–67% of referred students.

**Common Tier 2 Interventions:**

1. **Check-In/Check-Out (CICO):** Students meet with a coach at the start and end of each day to set behavior goals, receive feedback, and track progress. Research supports CICO as one of the most widely used and studied Tier 2 interventions.

2. **Small-group social skills instruction:** Targeted instruction in social skills (e.g., making friends, managing anger, problem-solving) delivered 2–3 times per week in a small group.

3. **Self-management groups:** Students learn to monitor and evaluate their own behavior, often using visual tracking systems or daily report cards.

4. **Academic support groups:** Small-group instruction in a specific skill (e.g., reading fluency, math computation) delivered by a specialist or teacher.

5. **Targeted mentoring or peer support:** A staff member or trained peer meets regularly with a student to provide encouragement and check on progress.

6. **Structured outdoor time or physical activity:** Regular, supervised physical activity with explicit behavioral expectations.

**Tier 2 in elementary schools—concrete examples:**

- **Morning group meeting:** A teacher or counselor meets with 8–10 identified students every morning (10 minutes) to discuss their daily goals, review expected behaviors, and practice a social skill.
- **Lunch bunch:** A staff member or volunteer eats lunch with a small group of students who are working on friendship skills or social anxiety, using structured activities and conversation.
- **Attendance check-in:** A staff member contacts families of students with emerging attendance patterns (4–5 absences) to understand barriers and problem-solve together.
- **Homework support club:** After school, 8–10 students receive structured support with homework and study skills in a calm, low-pressure setting.
- **Emotion regulation group:** Four to six students meet with a counselor twice a week to learn strategies for recognizing and managing strong emotions (deep breathing, body awareness, etc.).
- **Recess buddy program:** A student at risk for isolation is paired with a peer mentor and a staff member during recess to practice social skills in a supported setting.

**Equity in Tier 2:** Data on which students receive Tier 2 support must be reviewed for disproportionate representation. If students from a particular racial or ethnic group represent a higher percentage of Tier 2 participants than their percentage in the school, the focus should be on improving equity in Tier 1 systems first.

### Tier 3: Intensive Individual Interventions (≤ 5–10% of students)

**What it is:** Highly individualized, sustained interventions for students with ongoing, complex needs who have not adequately responded to Tier 1 and Tier 2 supports. Typically involves specialists, behavioral counselors, mental health professionals, and case management.

**Key characteristics:**
- Individualized to the student's unique needs
- May involve special education evaluation and services
- Frequent progress monitoring (weekly or more)
- Coordination across school, family, and community agencies
- May address trauma, mental health, housing, food insecurity, or other systemic barriers

## Georgia's MTSS Framework (GaMTSS)

Georgia has adapted MTSS under the name **Georgia Multi-Tiered System of Supports (GaMTSS)**, a statewide framework designed to help every student thrive. Georgia's framework explicitly integrates PBIS as a vital component and emphasizes a "whole child" approach.

### GaMTSS Core Components:

1. **Whole Child Support:** Academic learning, behavior, and wellbeing are all connected and central to success.

2. **Strong Foundations:** Districts establish leadership teams, clear procedures, and provide schools with tools, training, resources, assessments, coaching, and guidance on resource allocation.

3. **Teamwork:** Educators, staff, students, families, and community partners work together to solve problems and make data-informed decisions.

4. **Layers of Support:** Every student has access to high-quality instruction; those needing extra help receive targeted academic, behavioral, and wellbeing support.

5. **Data-Driven Decisions:** Data (test scores, behavior reports, wellbeing checks) are collected, reviewed, and used to set goals and monitor progress.

6. **Clear Expectations:** Schools define, teach, and model integrated academic, behavioral, and wellbeing expectations across all settings. For example, "respect" is taught as behavior in the classroom, hallway, and lunchroom, and connected to collaboration in academics and caring for self and others.

7. **Clear Behavior Definitions:** Schools adopt consistent definitions of behaviors, distinguishing classroom-managed from office-managed behaviors, supporting learning and self-regulation.

8. **Supportive Responses:** Staff respond to student needs with strategies that reteach skills, build relationships, and reduce exclusionary practices (e.g., suspensions).

9. **Positive School Climate:** Students and adults experience a learning community that is academically supportive, healthy, safe, where every child feels valued, connected, and respected.

### Recognition and Support:

- Georgia provides districts and schools with training, coaching, tools, and resources to support GaMTSS implementation.
- Over 120 districts with 1,450 schools and programs across Georgia include PBIS and/or GaMTSS.
- The state operates **GO MTSS/SST**, a web-based application for schools to document and monitor how students respond to interventions.
- Georgia receives federal **State Personnel Development Grant (SPDG)** funding (2022–2027) to improve adolescent literacy outcomes in grades 4–8 through GaMTSS implementation.

## School Connectedness and Belonging as Protective Factors

Research consistently shows that **school connectedness**—students' sense that adults and peers care about them, value them, and that they belong—is a powerful protective factor linked to academic, behavioral, and health outcomes.

### What CDC Research Shows:

The Centers for Disease Control defines school connectedness as: *Students feel that adults and peers in school care about the students as individuals and about their learning. This includes a sense of being cared for, being valued and supported, and belonging at school.*

**Youth who feel connected at school are less likely to experience:**
- Poor mental health
- Sexual health risks
- Substance use
- Violence
- Depression, suicidal ideation, and social anxiety

**Youth who feel connected at school are more likely to:**
- Engage in positive health behaviors (physical activity, healthy eating)
- Have higher grades and test scores
- Have better school attendance
- Graduate high school
- Experience positive long-term health and wellbeing outcomes into adulthood

**School connectedness has long-lasting effects.** Research shows that youth who feel connected at school are less likely to face sexual health risks, substance use, violence, and mental health challenges in adulthood—demonstrating that the benefits extend well beyond the school years.

**Equity considerations:** School connectedness is beneficial for all students, but not all students experience it equally. Students who report lower connectedness include those experiencing racism, students from racial and ethnic minority groups, and LGBTQ+ students. Schools play a key role in creating inclusive connectedness through strategies that reduce health disparities and promote belonging for all.

## The Protective Power of One Caring Adult

Research across multiple disciplines identifies **the presence of one stable, positive relationship with a caring adult** as a foundational protective factor for young people facing adversity, stress, or marginalization.

### Key Research Findings:

**The "One Caring Adult" Effect:** Multiple peer-reviewed studies document that youth with at least one caring, stable adult relationship show:
- Increased resilience in the face of challenges
- Reduced engagement in high-risk behaviors
- Better mental health outcomes
- Greater likelihood of thriving

**What defines a caring adult relationship:**
- Stability and dependability (someone the young person can trust)
- Genuine interest in the young person's life and wellbeing
- Availability for both celebration and problem-solving
- Advocacy (standing up for the young person when needed)
- High expectations combined with support

**Where caring adults come from:** In addition to parents and teachers, caring adults can include school counselors, coaches, mentors, community program leaders, faith leaders, relatives, and other consistent community members.

**Note on evidence:** While the research literature emphasizes the profound importance of at least one caring adult, most studies on this topic have focused on family members, school staff, and formal mentors. The evidence for out-of-school-time programs (including youth organizations) operating as a source of caring adult relationships is growing but less comprehensive. Programs that intentionally build developmental relationships—with training, supervision, and relationship-building practices—are positioned to serve this role, though more direct evaluation of specific programs is needed.

## Developmental Relationships Framework

**Search Institute** has developed and extensively researched the **Developmental Relationships Framework**, which identifies five evidence-based elements and 20 specific actions that help young people thrive. This framework is relevant because it describes how adults across all settings—including schools, programs, and community organizations—can intentionally build relationships that support young people's positive development.

### The Five Key Elements:

1. **Express Care** (Show me I matter to you)
   - Be dependable
   - Listen actively
   - Believe in me and make me feel known and valued
   - Show warmth and enjoyment in being together

2. **Challenge Growth** (Push me to keep getting better)
   - Encourage me and praise my efforts
   - Expect me to live up to my potential
   - Stretch me to go further
   - Hold me accountable and help me learn from failures

3. **Provide Support** (Help me complete tasks and achieve goals)
   - Navigate me through hard situations and systems
   - Empower me to take charge of my life
   - Advocate for me when I need it
   - Set boundaries that keep me on track

4. **Share Power** (Treat me with respect and give me a say)
   - Respect me and take me seriously
   - Include me in decisions that affect me
   - Collaborate with me to solve problems
   - Create opportunities for me to lead

5. **Expand Possibilities** (Connect me with people and places to broaden my world)
   - Inspire me to see possibilities for my future
   - Expose me to new ideas, experiences, and places
   - Connect me with people and networks who can help me grow

### Impact:

When young people experience these relationships in their families, schools, programs, and communities, they are more likely to be:
- Resilient in the face of challenges
- Engaged in increased academic growth and learning
- More socially and emotionally competent
- Responsible
- Less engaged in high-risk behaviors

**Note on measurement:** Search Institute has developed the **Developmental Relationships Survey**, which measures unseen factors that contribute to strong relationships between adults and youth. This tool helps organizations examine their practices and understand where relationships can be strengthened.

**Relevance to community programs:** While developmental relationships research originated in educational and family settings, the framework is being applied by youth-serving organizations, mentoring programs, and out-of-school-time programs. The research suggests that any adult working with young people—including community volunteers and program staff—can intentionally practice these elements.

## Chronic Absenteeism and Attendance Interventions

### What Chronic Absenteeism Is:

**Chronic absenteeism** is typically defined as a student missing 10% or more of school days in an academic year—equivalent to about 18 days in a 180-day school year. It is a significant predictor of academic failure, high school dropout, and long-term educational and employment outcomes.

### The Tiered Attendance Intervention Framework:

**Attendance Works**, a national organization focused on chronic absenteeism, recommends a tiered approach mirroring MTSS:

#### **Foundational Strategies (Whole School)**

Create positive conditions for learning where students are more likely to attend and be engaged:
- Welcoming, inclusive school climate
- Strong family and community partnerships
- Student and adult well-being support
- Accessible, equitable services (transportation, meals, etc.)

**Sources:**
- https://www.attendanceworks.org/chronic-absence/addressing-chronic-absence/3-tiers-of-intervention/
- https://www.attendanceworks.org/using-chronic-absence-data-to-improve-conditions-for-learning/

#### **Tier 1: Prevention and Universal Strategies**

Aimed at encouraging better attendance for all students and preventing absenteeism before it affects achievement:
- Clear attendance policies communicated to families
- Positive incentive programs recognizing good attendance
- Engaging instruction and school climate
- Early celebration of perfect or improved attendance (e.g., at school assemblies)

#### **Tier 2: Early Intervention (10% of students)**

Designed to remove barriers to attendance for students at greater risk, typically those who have missed 10% of school year:
- Personalized, data-informed outreach to families
- Problem-solving conversations to identify barriers (transportation, childcare, health, safety, engagement)
- Connection to resources and supports (food, medical services, counseling, transportation assistance)
- Mentoring or check-in support
- Documentation of barriers and solutions
- More frequent communication with family

**Examples of Tier 2 attendance interventions:**
- A staff member or mentor meets weekly with a student and family to discuss attendance barriers and progress.
- Transportation assistance is provided to a family with a recent move.
- A school social worker connects a family to community health services for a child with chronic illness.
- A student receives a positive phone call home for improved attendance.

#### **Tier 3: Intensive Support (Students missing 20%+)**

Intensive, individualized support often involving multiple agencies:
- Case management involving school, family, and community agencies
- Addressing underlying systemic barriers (homelessness, health problems, housing instability)
- Intensive mentoring or trusted adult relationship
- Court involvement if necessary (attendance officers, truancy proceedings)

**Sources:**
- https://www.attendanceworks.org/chronic-absence/addressing-chronic-absence/3-tiers-of-intervention/
- https://www.attendanceworks.org/chronic-absence/addressing-chronic-absence/what-activates-support-at-each-of-the-3-tiers/

## Mentoring and Check & Connect Programs for Attendance

**Check & Connect** is one of the most rigorously studied mentoring-based interventions for reducing school dropout and improving attendance. It is part of the Institute of Education Sciences (IES) "What Works Clearinghouse" and is classified as a program with evidence of effectiveness for dropout prevention.

### How Check & Connect Works:

The program uses two key components:

1. **Check:** A monitor or mentoring staff member regularly reviews information on attendance, behavior, and academic performance and intervenes when problems are identified.

2. **Connect:** The monitor provides individualized attention to students in partnership with school staff, families, and community organizations. The monitor:
   - Advocates for the student
   - Coordinates services
   - Provides ongoing feedback and encouragement
   - Emphasizes the importance of staying engaged in school
   - Problem-solves around barriers

### Evidence of Effectiveness:

Research from rigorous randomized controlled trials (RCTs) shows:
- Students in Check & Connect showed significant improvement in **staying in school** (staying enrolled rather than dropping out) compared to comparison groups.
- Effects were sustained over multiple years of follow-up.
- The program has been adapted and tested in multiple districts and school settings.
- Mentors in the program maintain relatively small caseloads (average 25 students) and meet with each student at least monthly (often more when problems arise), with mentors following students even if they transfer schools.

### Relevance to Attendance and Belonging:

Check & Connect's effectiveness suggests that:
- Regular monitoring by a trusted adult is protective.
- A consistent, caring adult relationship matters—mentors stay with students across transitions.
- Problem-solving around barriers (not just behavior management) is essential.
- Connection to broader school and community resources increases effectiveness.

## Out-of-School-Time Programs and Community Partnerships

### What Research Shows About OST Programs:

**Out-of-school time (OST) programs** are supervised, structured activities students attend outside school hours (after school, summer, before school). Recent research on promising practices for low-income youth identifies key elements linked to positive outcomes:

#### **Engagement Dimensions in Quality OST Programs:**

1. **Agency:** Young people have choice and voice in decision-making.
2. **Relevance:** Activities connect to real-life interests and goals.
3. **Contribution:** Programs foster community consciousness and leadership opportunities.
4. **Competence:** Activities progressively challenge youth with scaffolding support.
5. **Belonging:** Strong priority on relationships and cultural celebration; safe, inclusive environment.
6. **Exposure:** Field trips and novel experiences broaden horizons.
7. **Safety/Wellness:** Physical safety, access to food and resources, connections to needed services.

#### **Staffing Practices:**
- Hire staff with shared lived experiences when possible; emphasize openness and collaboration when differences exist.
- Provide training on both content and mentoring/relational practices.
- Establish strong leadership for retention and recruitment.
- Create pathways for program alumni to become staff.

#### **Recruitment and Engagement:**
- Leverage existing relationships with trusted community members.
- Communicate concrete benefits: skill-building and social connection.
- Address and remove barriers (transportation, cost) through accessible community locations.
- Build partnerships with trusted organizations.
- Use flexible attendance policies.
- Include culminating events and celebrations.

#### **Outcomes and Mechanisms:**

OST programs have produced improvements in:
- Social-emotional development
- STEM interest and achievement
- Academic achievement
- College aspirations
- Civic engagement

These outcomes operate through **positive youth development (PYD) frameworks**—building internal assets (competence, self-confidence, sense of purpose) and external assets (supportive relationships, community connections, access to resources).

### Community Partnerships in MTSS:

The research literature on MTSS and school connectedness emphasizes that:
- **Families and community partners are essential team members** in implementing MTSS. Georgia's GaMTSS explicitly calls for "teamwork" where educators, staff, students, families, and community partners work together.
- **Schools alone cannot address all barriers** to student success. Connection to community resources, health services, and supportive adults strengthens Tier 2 and Tier 3 interventions.
- **Trusted community organizations** can serve as sources of belonging, mentoring, and support, particularly when they are culturally responsive and prioritize relational practices.

**Note on evidence:** While research strongly supports the role of community partnerships in general and the benefits of out-of-school programs for youth development, the direct evidence linking specific community youth programs (like Cub Scouts) to MTSS tiers is emerging rather than definitive. The argument is theoretically sound—a well-designed youth program providing mentoring, skill-building, belonging, and connection to caring adults aligns with Tier 1 universal supports (belonging, positive climate) and Tier 2 targeted interventions (mentoring, social-emotional support, engagement strategies). However, this would ideally be supported by formal evaluation data specific to the program and outcomes of interest.

## Integration Points: How Community Programs Fit Into MTSS

Based on the research literature, here are the theoretically sound integration points for a community youth program (such as a Cub Scout pack) within a school's MTSS framework:

### **Tier 1: Universal Supports and School Connectedness**

A quality youth program can contribute to Tier 1 by:
- **Promoting belonging and connectedness:** Programs that prioritize relational practices, cultural celebration, and inclusive environments build students' sense of belonging.
- **Providing positive adult relationships:** Consistent adult volunteers/leaders create additional sources of caring relationships in students' lives.
- **Supporting positive skill development:** Age-appropriate challenges, leadership opportunities, and skill-building support the developmental assets literature identifies as protective.
- **Expanding possibilities:** Field trips, exposure to new experiences, and connections to community networks broaden students' worldviews and aspirations.

### **Tier 2: Targeted Interventions**

A community program can serve Tier 2 functions by:
- **Providing structured mentoring:** Regular, consistent contact with an adult mentor within the program setting addresses the research on protective mentoring relationships.
- **Offering small-group, targeted support:** Program activities can be designed to build specific social-emotional skills (teamwork, communication, resilience) needed by students.
- **Supporting attendance and engagement:** Engaging, relevant activities and mentoring relationships can motivate attendance at both the program and school.
- **Removing barriers:** Connection to community resources, transportation assistance, or food access can address concrete barriers to participation and engagement.
- **Check-in/check-out function:** Regular program meetings with a consistent adult can serve a similar function to school-based CICO—monitoring a student's wellbeing and progress and providing support.

### **Articulation with School Systems**

The effectiveness of community program participation within MTSS would be strengthened by:
- **Data sharing (with consent):** School awareness of a student's program participation, engagement, and progress could inform Tier 1 and Tier 2 decisions at school.
- **Communication about barriers and goals:** Open dialogue between program staff and school staff about a student's needs, interests, and progress supports coordinated support.
- **Alignment of expectations:** When school and program both emphasize similar behavioral/social-emotional expectations and use consistent language, students benefit.
- **Referral pathways:** Schools could refer students to programs as a Tier 2 or Tier 1 support (general recommendation for engagement and belonging).

**Important caveat on evidence:** The research base for youth programs as explicit MTSS interventions is growing but not yet as robust as the research for school-based interventions. Programs that invest in:
- Intentional relationship-building practices (aligned with developmental relationships framework)
- Staff training on mentoring and youth development
- Structured evaluation of student outcomes
- Clear communication with schools and families

...position themselves most strongly to document and strengthen their role as part of a student's overall support system.

## Domains Covered by MTSS

MTSS operates across multiple domains, all interconnected:

1. **Academic:** Quality core instruction, universal screening, targeted intervention in reading/math/writing, data-informed progress monitoring.

2. **Behavioral:** Teaching and reinforcing expectations, proactive classroom management, targeted social skills instruction, positive reinforcement systems, restorative practices.

3. **Social-Emotional:** Development of self-awareness, self-management, responsible decision-making, relationship skills, and social awareness. Examples include social skills groups, emotion regulation instruction, and conflict resolution.

4. **Attendance:** Foundational supports promoting positive school climate and engagement; early intervention for attendance patterns; intensive support for chronic absenteeism.

5. **Wellness/Wellbeing:** Student and adult mental health and physical health support, access to basic services (nutrition, medical), connections to community resources.

## Summary of Georgia's Role and Implications

Georgia's framework (GaMTSS) explicitly recognizes that:
- MTSS is a "whole child" endeavor requiring integration of academics, behavior, and wellbeing.
- **Family and community partnerships are essential**, not optional.
- Data-driven decision-making at each tier prevents students from falling through gaps.
- Equity is a core concern—schools must regularly examine whether supports are reaching all students equitably.
- Positive school climate and adult relationships are foundational to all other interventions.

For a school district considering how a community youth program fits its MTSS goals, the research suggests asking:
- Does the program build students' sense of belonging and connection to caring adults?
- Does it offer structured, developmentally appropriate support in social-emotional, behavioral, or academic domains?
- Does it align with school expectations and goals?
- Is there a communication pathway between program staff and school?
- Is the program accessible and equitable for all students?
- What outcomes data does the program collect, and how does it monitor fidelity?

## Limitations of Evidence and Recommendations for Further Exploration

This research synthesis draws on peer-reviewed literature, federal education guidance, and state frameworks. However:

1. **Community youth programs and MTSS:** While the theoretical alignment between quality youth programs and MTSS tiers is strong (especially around developmental relationships, mentoring, and belonging), the direct empirical evidence linking specific community programs like Cub Scouts to school MTSS outcomes is limited. Programs interested in demonstrating this alignment should consider:
   - Collecting outcome data aligned with MTSS domains (e.g., school attendance, social-emotional competency measures, sense of belonging).
   - Facilitating data-sharing partnerships with schools (with appropriate consent).
   - Evaluating implementation fidelity of developmental relationship practices.

2. **Equity:** While the research emphasizes that school connectedness and developmental relationships benefit all youth, research also highlights that some students—particularly those from marginalized communities—report lower connectedness. Programs should proactively assess and work to ensure equitable belonging and relationships for all participants.

3. **Attendance interventions:** The research on school-based attendance interventions is strong; community program contributions to attendance improvement would benefit from formal evaluation.

4. **Measurement:** Most cited research uses school-based measures (GPA, attendance, office referrals, graduation rates). Community programs may need to adapt or develop complementary measures that capture their unique contributions.
