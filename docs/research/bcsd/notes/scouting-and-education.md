# Scouting and Education: Research Notes for a School District Conversation

Prepared for Pack 170 (Macon, Georgia) to inform a conversation with Bibb County School District (BCSD)
about whether a Cub Scout pack is an asset or a liability to a school. Research only; no advocacy
claims are made without a cited source. Every claim below is tagged by evidence strength — see the
table near the end.

## 1. Research on outcomes for youth in Scouting

### 1.1 Tufts University / Institute for Applied Research in Youth Development — Cub Scout longitudinal study (peer-reviewed)

**Study:** Wang, J., Ferris, K. A., Hershberg, R. M., & Lerner, R. M. (2015). "Developmental
Trajectories of Youth Character: A Five-Wave Longitudinal Study of Cub Scouts and Non-Scout Boys."
_Journal of Youth and Adolescence_, 44(12). DOI: 10.1007/s10964-015-0340-y.
Source: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26280400/

- **Author/institution:** Tufts University Institute for Applied Research in Youth Development
  (Richard M. Lerner's lab), part of the BSA/Tufts "Character and Merit Project."
- **Sample:** 1,398 Cub Scouts (mean age 8.59) and 325 non-Scout boys (mean age 9.06), five waves of
  self-report survey data over 2.5 years.
- **Method:** Latent growth-curve analysis of self-report ratings on cheerfulness, helpfulness,
  kindness, obedience, trustworthiness, reverence, thriftiness, school performance, and hopeful
  future expectations.
- **Findings:** Scouts' self-ratings increased significantly over time for cheerfulness, helpfulness,
  kindness, obedience, trustworthiness, and hopeful future expectations. Non-Scout boys showed no
  significant change on these measures (and a significant _decrease_ in religious reverence for
  non-Scouts from religious institutions).
- **Limitations to disclose to a school audience:**
  - This is **not a randomized trial**. Scouts and comparison boys were not randomly assigned;
    families who enroll a child in Scouting likely differ systematically from those who don't
    (self-selection).
  - All outcome measures are **child self-report**, not independent/objective measures (no grades,
    attendance records, or teacher ratings).
  - The comparison group (325 boys) is much smaller than the Scout sample (1,398).
  - Funded/facilitated in partnership with BSA (the "Character and Merit Project" was a BSA-Tufts
    collaboration), which is a conflict of interest to disclose even though it ran through peer
    review.
- A related Tufts/BSA study specifically on the **Scoutreach** initiative (low-income, racially and
  ethnically diverse youth) found similar self-reported character gains: Wang, J., Champine, R. B.,
  Ferris, K. A., et al. (2017). "Is the Scoutreach Initiative of Boy Scouts of America Linked to
  Character Development among Socioeconomically, Racially, and Ethnically Diverse Youth?" _Journal of
  Youth and Adolescence_, 46(10), 2230–2240. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28664312/ (same
  self-report/non-randomized limitations apply).

### 1.3 Harris Poll / Harris Interactive studies commissioned by (Scouting America (formerly BSA)) — organization-commissioned

- **The Scouting Edge: A Study of Ethics and Character in America** — conducted by Scouting America
  in partnership with the Harris Poll; compares Scouts/alumni to non-Scouts on self-reported ethics,
  values, and civic attitudes. https://www.scouting.org/about/research/the-scouting-edge-study/
  (PDF: https://filestore.scouting.org/filestore/marketing/pdf/Final-File-The-Scouting-Edge-8-5x11.pdf)
  - Findings are entirely self-reported opinion/attitude survey data (e.g., Scouts/alumni "more
    likely" to call various behaviors wrong, more likely to say voting/volunteering matters).
    No behavioral or administrative-record outcomes.
- **Eagle Scouts: A Legacy of Values-Based Leadership and Service (2026)** — Scouting America
  commissioned The Harris Poll; online survey fielded October 10, 2025–January 2026; **n = 3,178
  adults 18+**, including 1,549 non-Scouts, 1,067 Eagle Scouts, and 562 non-Eagle Scouts. Released at
  the Scouting America National Annual Meeting (May 2026).
  PDF: https://filestore.scouting.org/filestore/marketing/pdf/Eagle%20Scout%20Study%202026.pdf
  Summary coverage: https://onscouting.org/2026/06/04/study-shows-eagle-scouts-have-a-head-start-to-a-happy-life/
  - Reported findings: Eagle Scouts report higher daily enjoyment (86% vs. 68% for non-Scouts),
    gratitude (84% vs. 73%), sense of purpose (78% vs. 60%), lower loneliness (11% vs. 23%,
    "even after controlling for income, education, age and other demographic factors" per BSA's own
    description — the underlying regression model is not published in full), and better self-reported
    health behaviors.
  - **Method is cross-sectional, retrospective, adult self-report**, commissioned and paid for by the
    same organization whose program is being evaluated, and it compares _people who stayed in
    Scouting long enough to reach Eagle_ against people who never joined — a strong self-selection
    and survivorship bias (a plausible confound: kids who persist to Eagle rank may already have
    more supportive families, more stable households, or more of the very traits — grit,
    persistence — being "found" in the results).
  - The earlier "Merit Beyond the Badges" 2010 Eagle Scout study description is referenced within
    this same PDF; it's the immediate predecessor of this 2026 wave.

### 1.5 Other peer-reviewed work on Scouting and character/persistence

- Callina, K. S., Johnson, S. K., Tirrell, J. M., Batanova, M., Weiner, M. B., & Lerner, R. M. (2017).
  "Modeling Pathways of Character Development across the First Three Decades of Life…" _Journal of

  Tufts Scout dataset to look at hopeful future expectations across decades; same self-report/

- No peer-reviewed study located that uses **independent school records** (grades, attendance,
  disciplinary data) as the outcome variable for Cub-Scout-age children. This is a real gap — be
  candid with the district that the "improves grades/attendance" claim is currently an inference
  from character/behavior self-report studies, not a study of school records.

## 2. History of Scouting and schools in the United States

- **Chartered-organization model, from the start.** Scouting America has never owned its own meeting
  space; from the 1910 founding it has operated by chartering community organizations — churches,
  civic clubs, and schools among them — to sponsor units. Congress granted BSA a federal charter on
  June 15, 1916 (36 U.S.C. § 30902), formalizing the national organization but not creating any
  special government control. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scouting_America ;
  Chartered Organization Resource Guide (BSA):
  https://scoutingwire.org/wp-content/uploads/2016/05/Chartered-Organization-Resource-Guide_522-925_wb.pdf
- **Schools as chartered organizations.** Public and private schools have long been eligible
  chartered organizations, and PTAs/school-affiliated groups have historically sponsored Cub Scout
  packs meeting on school property — this is the same legal/organizational mechanism a church or
  Rotary club uses, not a separate "school program." (Chartered Organization Resource Guide, above.)
- **The largest single sponsor was a religious body, not a school system.** The Church of Jesus
  Christ of Latter-day Saints adopted Scouting in 1913 as its young-men's program and was the single
  largest chartering organization until it ended its Scouting affiliation at the close of 2019 —
  illustrating how concentrated chartering has been in faith communities relative to schools.
  https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scouting_America
- **2000s-era shift and decline in school-chartered units.** Litigation and advocacy pressure in the
  2000s (over BSA's religious-belief and, later, its historical gender/orientation membership
  policies) led several public school districts and municipalities to sever or reconsider
  school-based chartering and in-kind support because of Establishment Clause and equal-access
  concerns; the practical effect nationally was a shift of school-based packs toward PTA-adjacent or
  independent community-organization charters rather than the school itself holding the charter. This
  shift is documented piecemeal in litigation history (e.g., _Winkler v. Chicago School Reform Board_,
  a 2004 case on a Chicago public school's continued sponsorship) rather than in a single consolidated
  BSA history document. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scouting_America_membership_controversies
  (see "Access to Government Forums" and school-sponsorship citations). Treat this as a documented
  legal/organizational trend, not a single dated policy change.
- **Current in-school/underserved-community delivery model: Scoutreach.** Scouting America's current
  program for delivering Scouting inside schools and after-school settings, particularly in
  lower-income and historically underserved communities, is **Scoutreach**. Local councils operate it
  as direct-service den meetings held during or immediately after the school day, often staffed by a
  paid council program executive rather than an all-volunteer model. Examples: Long Beach Area Council
  ScoutReach — 27 schools, three parks, one community center, 782 youth, current school year
  (https://www.longbeachbsa.org/activities/scoutreach/); Greater Los Angeles Area Council
  (https://ggacbsa.org/scout-reach/); Heart of Virginia Council
  (https://hovc.org/ways-to-give/scoutreach/). A 2017 Tufts/BSA study specifically evaluated Scoutreach
  participants' character development (see §1.5 and §1.1 above).
- **STEM Scouts** was a separate BSA-run, classroom/school-adjacent STEM program (distinct from the
  Cub Scout Nova awards, see §3 below); it operated as an affiliate program rather than the standard
  den/pack model. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scouting_America (STEM Scouts citation).
- **Georgia/Southeast specifics:** No Georgia- or Southeast-specific historical account of
  school-chartered Scouting was found in this pass beyond the general national pattern above. If this
  matters for the district conversation, a targeted follow-up query to the local council (Central
  Georgia Council) or Bibb County school board archives would be needed — flag this as **not yet
  researched**, not as "no such history exists."

## 3. How Cub Scouting content maps to school priorities

Cub Scouting's four **Aims** — Character, Citizenship, Leadership, and Personal Fitness — are
delivered through the **Methods** of the Den (small group), the ideals (Scout Oath/Law/motto/slogan),
Advancement (Adventures), Uniform, and adult association, per Scouting America's own program
literature (https://www.scouting.org/programs/cub-scouts/). Below is the grade-by-grade structure,
sourced directly from the current official requirements pages.

Every rank's required-adventure set follows the same six pillars — Character & Leadership, Personal
Fitness, Personal Safety, Family & Reverence, Citizenship, Outdoors — confirmed directly on the
official Tiger and Webelos pages scraped above; the pattern repeats (with age-appropriate content)
at Lion, Wolf, and Bear per the program's own design description.

**Concrete school-relevant mappings:**

- **Citizenship / civics:** Every rank has a required Citizenship-pillar adventure (e.g., "Team
  Tiger," "My Community") — directly parallel to elementary social-studies civics standards.
- **Personal Safety:** Every rank includes a required child-abuse-prevention component (the Bobcat
  adventure explicitly requires the parent guide on child abuse) — this is worth surfacing to a
  district worried about youth-safety risk, since the curriculum itself is built around it, not
  bolted on.
- **STEM:** BSA's national **STEM Nova and Super Nova Awards** program (science/technology/
  engineering/math modules layered on top of standard advancement) is being **discontinued as a
  national program effective June 1, 2025**, per multiple council announcements
  (https://scoutingphilly.org/leaders/stem/ ;
  https://www.facebook.com/CommissionersOfScoutingAmerica/posts/stem-nova-and-super-nova-award-updatessignificant-changes-to-the-stem-nova-and-s/659436093122780/).
  **This is a current-state correction to flag**: do not tell the district Nova awards are an active
  national program without checking current local-council status — some councils may continue
  locally-run equivalents, but the national award structure is being wound down.
- **Literacy/public speaking:** Elective adventures such as "Stories in Shapes" (Tiger) and den
  meeting structures that require Scouts to report out to the group are the closest curricular analog;
  no standardized "public speaking" adventure was identified by name in this pass.
- **Outdoor/physical activity:** Every rank has a required Personal Fitness adventure and a required
  Outdoors adventure (e.g., "Tigers in the Wild," "Webelos Walkabout").
- **Den structure as small-group leadership practice:** The den (roughly 6–8 Scouts of the same
  grade, led by a rotating parent den leader with youth taking on roles like Denner/Assistant Denner)
  is the basic organizational unit beneath the pack — structurally similar to a classroom small-group
  or table-group model, and is one of BSA's core "Methods" alongside advancement, uniforming, and
  adult association (https://www.scouting.org/programs/cub-scouts/).

## 4. The risk side, honestly

### 4.1 Youth protection requirements (current, as of this research)

- **Two-deep leadership:** Two registered adult leaders, both at least 21, are required at all
  Scouting activities including meetings; one must be a registered female adult leader if females are
  present in the unit (rules vary by exact activity type). Effective **September 1, 2023** update.
  https://www.scouting.org/health-and-safety/gss/gss01/ ;
  https://scoutingnlc.org/updates-to-youth-protection-standards/?amp=1
- **No one-on-one contact** between an adult and a youth who is not their own child/ward is prohibited
  under the same Youth Protection policies (https://www.trcscouts.org/landing/youth-protection-training/).
- **Youth Protection Training (YPT)** is mandatory for all registered adult leaders and **must be
  renewed** (BSA policy has required annual renewal in recent years; confirm exact current cadence
  with the local council, as BSA has adjusted renewal intervals over time).
- **Criminal background checks** are required for all registered adult leaders as part of the
  national YPT/registration process.
- These are current, national, mandatory policies — not aspirational guidance — and they predate and
  are separate from council-by-council practice.

### 4.2 Abuse history and the bankruptcy — do not minimize

- Scouting America (then Boy Scouts of America) filed for **Chapter 11 bankruptcy in February 2020**,
  driven overwhelmingly by an enormous volume of historical child sexual abuse claims that had
  accumulated as states opened "lookback windows" allowing previously time-barred claims.
- The bankruptcy resolved with a **Plan of Reorganization confirmed in 2022**, establishing a
  **Scouting Settlement Trust funded at roughly $2.4 billion** to compensate survivors — one of the
  largest sexual-abuse compensation funds of its kind in U.S. history. **More than 85% of survivors
  voting on the plan supported it.**
  https://www.scoutingnewsroom.org/press-releases/more-than-85-of-survivors-support-the-boy-scouts-of-america-bsas-plan-of-reorganization/
  https://www.scoutingnewsroom.org/press-releases/the-boy-scouts-of-america-bsa-announces-confirmation-of-plan-of-reorganization-and-emergence-from-chapter-11-bankruptcy-to-equitably-compensate-survivors-while-ensuring-scouting-continues-across-the/
- The plan was contested by some survivors who felt compensation was inadequate; a federal district
  court upheld the $2.4B plan, and the **U.S. Supreme Court declined to review it**, leaving the
  settlement in place.
  https://www.pbs.org/newshour/nation/judge-upholds-boy-scouts-2-4-billion-bankruptcy-reorganization-plan ;
  https://www.wvtm13.com/article/supreme-court-boy-scouts-bankruptcy-settlement/69976327
- Ongoing claims administration is handled by the independent **Scouting Settlement Trust**
  (https://www.scoutingsettlementtrust.com/s/), which continues processing claims and selling
  designated local-council real property to fund the trust — this is not a closed, historical matter;
  it is still actively administering payouts.
- **What changed as a result, concretely:** the modern two-deep leadership/no-one-on-one-contact/
  mandatory YPT/background-check regime described in §4.1 is the direct organizational response to
  this history, tightened multiple times over the past two decades and again in 2023. A district
  should be told plainly: the abuse happened at scale, it was catastrophic, the bankruptcy was a
  direct consequence, and the current youth-protection architecture exists _because of_ that history,
  not despite it.

### 4.3 Insurance and liability

- Scouting America carries a **national general liability program covering all chartered
  organizations on file**, providing primary coverage (reported at **$1M per occurrence / $10M
  aggregate**, with excess layers above that — e.g., a first excess layer around $6.5M/occurrence,
  $13M/aggregate per one council's summary document) for liability arising from unit activities.
  https://www.scouting.org/health-and-safety/gss/gss10/ ;
  https://tampabayscouting.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/06/BSA-General-Liability-In-surance-Coverage-for-Chartered-Affiliate-Organizations.pdf
- This coverage is funded through a portion of the **annual national membership fee**, not a separate
  bill to the chartered organization — meaning a school chartering (or hosting) a unit is not itself
  purchasing or underwriting the insurance.
  https://www.capitolareascouting.org/resources/insurance/
- **Unit charter fee** (paid to the local council, not the school) has historically been modest —
  e.g., increased from $20/year to $40/year per unit in a 2012 national fee change
  (https://onscouting.org/2012/09/10/unit-charter-fee-changes-how-whats-new-will-affect-you/); current
  fees should be confirmed with the local council, as national fees have changed since.

### 4.4 Cost to a school

- A chartering/hosting school's direct cost is typically **meeting-space use only** (no rent
  obligation in the standard chartered-organization relationship — the organization "owns" the
  program much like a franchise but is not required to pay BSA); national insurance is funded by
  member fees as above. No study or BSA document was found quantifying district-wide facilities cost
  (custodial time, after-hours building access) — this is a reasonable operational question for a
  district to ask that this research did not resolve; flag as **not yet answered**, not zero.

### 4.5 Non-discrimination and membership policy — current state

- Scouting America's stated policy: _"It is the philosophy of Scouting to welcome all eligible youth,
  regardless of race, ethnic background, gender or orientation, who are willing to accept Scouting's
  values and meet any other requirements of membership."_
  https://www.scouting.org/about/membership-policy/
- **Girls:** Girls were officially admitted to **all** Scouting America programs, from Cub Scouts
  through Eagle Scout, nationwide, effective **February 1, 2019**.
  https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scouting_America_membership_controversies
- **Faith:** Scouting requires acknowledgment of a "Duty to God" concept broadly (not tied to a
  specific religion), and individual chartered organizations retain discretion over religious content
  and, historically, over specific membership standards tied to their own faith tradition
  (https://troopleader.scouting.org/information-duty-to-god/). Atheist/agnostic youth membership has
  been a live tension historically; the national organization's default policy text does not exclude
  them outright but chartered organizations may set additional expectations
  (see https://scoutermom.com/16177/faith-requirements-for-cub-scouts/, a secondary source, cited here
  only as illustrative of the ongoing ambiguity, not as an authoritative policy statement).
- **A live, current, and contested policy change:** In **early-to-mid 2026**, Scouting America reached
  an agreement with the U.S. Department of Defense/Pentagon (announced by Defense Secretary Pete
  Hegseth) intended to preserve DoD's institutional support relationship with Scouting. Reported terms
  include Scouting America agreeing to frame membership around **"biological sex at birth" rather than
  gender identity**. Scouting America's own public statement disputes that this changes its existing
  transgender-youth policies, stating trans youth remain welcome; the practical policy language and
  its legal force are **actively disputed** — a lawsuit/FOIA complaint was filed in mid-2026 seeking to
  clarify exactly what was agreed to.
  AP: https://apnews.com/article/scouting-america-pentagon-military-boy-scouts-14a5fc1521fcd1e51103638f6f504214 ;
  PBS: https://www.pbs.org/newshour/nation/transgender-youths-are-targeted-in-scouting-america-changes-pushed-by-the-pentagon ;
  ongoing dispute: https://www.wsls.com/news/politics/2026/06/26/complaint-seeks-answers-on-scouting-americas-transgender-policy-after-pentagon-deal/
  **This is unresolved and should be presented to the district as an open, contested, current-events
  item, not a settled fact** — the organization's public statement and the Pentagon's characterization
  of the same agreement conflict.
- **Adult leaders:** Scouting America's current national policy allows chartered organizations to
  select adult leaders without regard to sexual orientation for that specific chartered organization's
  units (a policy in place since 2015 and reaffirmed since) — see
  https://www.scouting.org/about/membership-standards/.

### 4.6 Religious-affiliation questions given church-chartered units

- Because many units nationally are chartered by churches (historically dominated by LDS chartering
  until it exited in 2019, per §2), a district partner should understand that **a school-chartered or
  school-hosted pack is organizationally distinct from a church-chartered one** — the chartered
  organization (school, church, civic club) sets the specific meeting-space and local relationship
  terms, while national Scouting America policy (membership, youth protection, insurance) applies
  uniformly regardless of charter type. A school considering hosting or chartering Pack 170 would not
  be adopting any particular unit's prior religious charter history.

## 5. Family/community engagement evidence — mapped to BCSD's Goal 3 (Stakeholder Engagement)

BCSD's strategic-plan Goal 3 treats stakeholder engagement (3.1 Collective Ownership, 3.2 Mutual
Trust & Relationship-Building, 3.3 Maximizing Student Learning) as an _input_ to achievement. This
section separates (a) established general research on family/community engagement and achievement,
from (b) the specific, largely un-tested argument that a Cub Scout pack is a good vehicle for that
engagement.

### 5.1 Family involvement and achievement — the general research base (peer-reviewed synthesis)

- **Henderson, A. T., & Mapp, K. L. (2002). _A New Wave of Evidence: The Impact of School, Family,
  and Community Connections on Student Achievement._ Southwest Educational Development Laboratory
  (SEDL), National Center for Family & Community Connections with Schools.**
  Full text: https://sedl.org/connections/resources/evidence.pdf (also
  https://massinc.org/wp-content/uploads/2014/01/evidence.pdf ;
  ERIC record: https://eric.ed.gov/?id=ED474521)
  - **Method:** Synthesis/review of more than 50 research studies published since 1995, building on
    Henderson & Berla's earlier "evidence" reports going back to the 1980s.
  - **Findings:** "The evidence is consistent, positive, and convincing: families have a major
    influence on their children's achievement in school and through life." Family involvement is
    linked to gains in reading and math achievement, and to improved attendance and behavior — not
    just test scores. Effects hold across income and education levels, though the report also notes
    that white, middle-class families tend to be more involved at school by default, meaning
    _increasing_ involvement from all family types (not just relying on existing high-involvement
    families) is an equity-relevant strategy for closing gaps.
  - **Limitation to disclose:** This is a synthesis of correlational school-based studies, not a
    single randomized experiment; it establishes a strong, consistent association between family
    involvement and achievement/attendance/behavior, not a controlled causal estimate of any single
    intervention type (including Scouting, which is not examined in this report at all).
- **Epstein, J. L. — Six Types of Parental Involvement framework** (parenting, communicating,
  volunteering, learning at home, decision-making, collaborating with the community). Widely used
  school-family-partnership model.
  https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/education/joyce-epsteins-school-family-community-partnership-model ;
  practitioner summary: https://www.ocecd.org/Downloads/Epstein%27s%20Framework%20-%20six%20times%20of%20involvement%2020163.pdf
  - Epstein's framework explicitly distinguishes _volunteering_ and _collaborating with the
    community_ as two of the six legitimate, evidence-informed channels of engagement — this gives a
    school-side, non-Scouting-specific vocabulary a district partnership office is likely already
    using, which a pack-district conversation can map onto directly (see §5.4).

### 5.2 Social capital between families and schools (theoretical, empirically supported at the level of the general framework, not Scouting-specific)

- **Coleman, J. S. (1988), and Putnam, R. D. (1995, 2000, _Bowling Alone_)** established the concept
  of social capital — trust, norms, and networks among people that make collective action (including
  supporting children's schooling) more effective. Putnam's own work documents a decades-long decline
  in exactly the kind of civic-association membership (bowling leagues, PTAs, and by extension youth
  groups like Scouting) that historically built this capital.
  https://www.familiesandschools.org/blog/social-capital/ (secondary summary with citations to
  Putnam 1995/2000) ; https://infed.org/dir/welcome/social-capital/ (secondary summary of Coleman and
  Putnam) ; primary Putnam article: https://www.cftompkins.org/wp-content/uploads/2012/07/Putnam-article.pdf
- **Evidence strength note:** This is a well-established _theoretical framework_ with substantial
  empirical support at the level of "civic associational density correlates with community
  well-being and school functioning." It is **not** a study that measured whether a Cub Scout pack
  specifically builds social capital between families and a school. Using it in a district
  conversation is legitimate as a well-supported _argument_ — "structured, recurring, parent-run
  organizations are a documented mechanism for building the trust and norms that Coleman/Putnam
  describe" — but it should be labeled as argument-from-theory, not a Scouting-specific finding.

### 5.3 Adult volunteer pipeline — cost and difficulty of school volunteer recruitment/vetting

- **Direct research on "does volunteering in one youth setting predict volunteering/involvement in
  another" (e.g., Scout parent → school volunteer) was not located in this pass.** This causal claim
  should be presented to the district as a **plausible argument, not a measured finding**: a Cub
  Scout pack requires each family to produce at least one background-checked, Youth-Protection-trained
  adult (per BSA's two-deep leadership rule, §4.1), which is structurally the same vetting schools
  themselves require of volunteers — so the pack is, in effect, pre-qualifying a pool of vetted adults
  who already have a demonstrated recent record of showing up for a children's program.
- **What is documented: recruiting and vetting school volunteers is itself costly and difficult for
  districts**, which is exactly why a pre-vetted adult pool has real value:
  - Districts report per-volunteer background-check costs ranging roughly from **$9.75 to $74.50 per
    person** depending on the screening tier and vendor, per a survey of district figures (Duval
    County Schools reported at $74.50 for Level II screening).
    https://www.volunteerbadge.com/blog/school-volunteer-background-check
  - Williamson County Schools (TN) charged **$40** for Tier 3 (unsupervised-contact) volunteers as of
    2012 reporting. https://www.edweek.org/education/volunteering-at-school-first-pay-for-your-background-check/2012/05
  - Indianapolis Public Schools moved to requiring volunteers to **pay for their own background
    checks**, illustrating districts passing along a cost they otherwise absorb.
    https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=P45S1_ZLG0g
  - These sources are journalism/trade-press reporting on district practice, not peer-reviewed
    research, but they are direct, dated, and specific — treat as (c) plausible-argument-supporting
    facts, useful for cost framing, not as an academic causal study.

### 5.4 Community-school partnership literature: what a school gains from hosting a chartered organization

- The relevant general literature (community-school models, Epstein's "collaborating with the
  community" category, §5.1) supports the general proposition that schools benefit from formal
  partnerships with community organizations that bring structured adult supervision, enrichment
  programming, and family engagement into or adjacent to the building — but this research pass did
  not locate a study specifically quantifying the benefit of a **chartered youth organization**
  (Scouts, 4-H, or similar) operating in a school building, as distinct from broader community-school
  partnership models (health clinics, food pantries, after-school tutoring) that dominate that
  literature. **This is a gap**: the district conversation should rely on the general
  family-engagement evidence (§5.1–5.2) plus the concrete, verifiable facts about Scouting's youth
  protection and insurance structure (§4.1, §4.3) as the basis for the specific proposal, rather than
  citing a nonexistent study of "Scout packs in schools" causing achievement gains.
