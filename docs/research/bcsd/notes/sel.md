# Social-Emotional Learning (SEL) Research: Supporting Cub Scout Advocacy to Georgia Schools

## CASEL Framework: Five Core Competencies

According to CASEL (Collaborative for Academic, Social, and Emotional Learning), SEL is built on five interrelated competence areas:

1. **Self-Awareness**: The capacity to identify and comprehend one's own feelings, ideas, and values as well as how they affect behavior.

2. **Self-Management**: The ability to manage emotions, control impulses, and set and work toward personal and academic goals.

3. **Social Awareness**: The ability to understand and empathize with others' perspectives, experiences, and emotions; to take others' viewpoints and demonstrate concern for others' well-being.

4. **Relationship Skills**: The capacity to build and maintain healthy, fulfilling relationships with diverse individuals and groups, including communicating clearly, listening actively, cooperating, resisting inappropriate social pressure, negotiating conflict constructively, and seeking or offering help when needed.

5. **Responsible Decision-Making**: The ability to make constructive and respectful choices about personal behavior and social interactions based on consideration of ethical standards, safety concerns, social norms, the realistic evaluation of consequences, and the well-being of oneself and others.

*Source: [CASEL Framework](https://casel.org/casel-sel-framework-11-2020/)*

### Where SEL Happens: A Systemic Approach

CASEL emphasizes that SEL occurs not in a single setting but across a coordinated ecosystem. The framework identifies four key environments:

- **Classrooms** – through explicit instruction and daily practice
- **Schools** – through school-wide policies, school climate, and adult modeling
- **Homes** – through family engagement and parental support
- **Communities** – through partnerships with community organizations, youth programs, mentoring, and out-of-school activities

CASEL stresses "the importance of establishing equitable learning environments and coordinating practices" across these four environments. This systemic view means that community organizations and out-of-school-time (OST) programs play an essential role in students' SEL development, not as supplements to school-based SEL, but as complementary partners in a coordinated system.

*Sources: [CASEL Systemic Implementation](https://casel.org/systemic-implementation/), [SEL in Communities](https://casel.org/systemic-implementation/sel-in-communities/)*

## Evidence Base: Meta-Analyses and Key Studies

### Durlak et al. (2011) – The Foundational Meta-Analysis

**Study Design:**
- **Sample**: 213 school-based universal SEL programs
- **Participants**: 270,034 kindergarten through high school students
- **Published**: Child Development, January–February 2011

**Key Findings on Academic Achievement:**
- SEL participants achieved an **11-percentile-point gain in academic achievement** compared to controls
- **Effect size for academic achievement**: g = 0.27 (standardized mean difference)
- **Overall effect size across all SEL domains**: g = 0.57
- Academic outcomes included standardized test scores, GPA, and academic performance measures

**Additional Outcomes:**
- **Social-emotional skills**: Significantly enhanced
- **Attitudes**: Positive shifts (e.g., school bonding, self-esteem)
- **Positive social behavior**: Increased cooperation, empathy, and prosocial problem-solving
- **Conduct problems**: Significantly reduced (disruptive behavior, fighting, bullying)
- **Emotional distress**: Significantly reduced (depression, anxiety, stress symptoms)

**Implementation Factors:**
The study found that programs using four recommended skill-building practices achieved stronger outcomes; however, only 16% of studies collected information on academic achievement, and the authors called for longer follow-up investigations to confirm durability of impact.

*Source: [Durlak et al. 2011, Child Development](https://srcd.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1467-8624.2010.01564.x)*

### Taylor et al. (2017) – Long-Term Effects Meta-Analysis

**Study Design:**
- **Sample**: 82 school-based universal SEL interventions
- **Participants**: 97,406 kindergarten to high school students (mean age 11.09 years; 41.1% low SES; 45.9% students of color)
- **Follow-up periods**: 6 months to 18 years post-intervention
- **Published**: Child Development, 88(4), 1156–1171

**Key Findings:**
- SEL program participants showed **significantly better outcomes in social-emotional skills, attitudes, and indicators of well-being** at follow-up
- **Mean effect size for academic achievement**: 0.26 to 0.33 (comparable to the Durlak 2011 finding of an 11 percentile-point gain)
- **Critical predictor of persistence**: The strength of short-term SEL gains was the best predictor of long-term gains, indicating that substantial immediate skill development tends to persist longer

**Equity Finding:**
Benefits were **equitable across demographic groups**—similar effect sizes regardless of students' race/ethnicity, socioeconomic background, or school location.

**Notable Outcomes:**
The study identified infrequently assessed but important long-term outcomes including graduation rates and safe sexual behavior, illustrating SEL's potential to improve critical developmental trajectories.

*Sources: [Taylor et al. 2017, Child Development](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/cdev.12864); [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28685826/)*

### Mahoney, Durlak, and Weissberg (2018–2019) Update on SEL Research

**Study Design:**
- **Scope**: Compared results from four large-scale meta-analyses
- **Research reports analyzed**: 356 with rigorous designs
- **Sample size**: Hundreds of thousands of K-12 students within and outside the United States
- **Published**: Phi Delta Kappan, 100(4), 18–23 (2018); also ERIC 2019

**Key Findings:**
Universal, school-based SEL programs produce positive benefits for participating students across:
- Academic outcomes
- Behavioral outcomes (conduct, social behavior)
- Emotional outcomes (reduced anxiety, depression, distress)
- Immediate post-intervention results and sustained effects at follow-up periods

**Critical Conclusion:**
"Current data indicate that SEL programs are both feasible and effective in a variety of educational contexts in many countries around the world."

The review emphasizes that benefits persist during various follow-up periods, meaning the effects are not transient.

*Source: [An Update on Social and Emotional Learning Outcome Research](https://kappanonline.org/social-emotional-learning-outcome-research-mahoney-durlak-weissberg/)*

## Cost-Benefit Analysis

### Belfield et al. (2015) – Economic Value of SEL

**Study Design:**
- **Authors**: Clive Belfield, A. Brooks Bowden, Alli Klapp, Henry Levin, Robert Shand, Sabine Zander
- **Organization**: Center for Benefit-Cost Studies of Education, Teachers College, Columbia University
- **Published**: Journal of Benefit-Cost Analysis, 6, 508–544 (2015)

**Methodology:**
The study used benefit-cost analysis (BCA) to estimate economic returns of six different SEL interventions. Benefits were estimated through shadow pricing, assigning monetary values to improved outcomes such as:
- Enhanced social competence
- Improved school climate
- Reduced bullying and drug use
- Increased academic achievement
- Benefits calculated as future lifetime earnings gains for students

**Key Finding:**
- **Benefit-Cost Ratio: 11:1**
  - For every $1.00 invested in a SEL program, the economic return is approximately $11.00
  - This represents both short-term benefits (improved school climate, reduced behavior problems) and long-term benefits (higher lifetime earnings due to improved academic and behavioral outcomes)

**Methodological Note:**
The authors acknowledge that benefit-cost analysis of SEL presents challenges because monetary values can be estimated for only some outcomes (they estimated monetary benefits for 3 of 14 outcomes). Even with these conservative limitations, "the benefits of these interventions substantially outweigh the costs."

*Sources: [Belfield et al. 2015](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-benefit-cost-analysis/article/economic-value-of-social-and-emotional-learning/D9A12352A7CF1B39E9A2B7EA4C68F150); [Center for Benefit-Cost Studies of Education](https://www.cbcse.org/publications/the-economic-value-of-social-and-emotional-learning)*

## Georgia-Specific SEL Policy and Standards

### Georgia's SEL Framework Alignment

Georgia has adopted a SEL framework aligned with CASEL's five core competencies. Key components include:

**Early Learning Standards:**
- The **Georgia Early Learning and Development Standards (GELDS)**, administered by the Georgia Department of Early Care and Learning (DECAL), define healthy development from birth through kindergarten entry
- **Social-emotional development is designated as a core domain** of GELDS, with standards for children ages birth through 5 years that include competencies such as:
  - Demonstrating self-control
  - Developing relationships with adults
  - Developing relationships with peers
  - Managing emotions

*Source: [Georgia Early Learning and Development Standards](https://gelds.decal.ga.gov/)*

**K-12 School Climate Initiatives:**
- Georgia's Department of Education Office of Whole Child Supports provides resources and technical assistance to districts and schools to create safe, positive learning environments
- School climate is explicitly named as a priority in the GaDOE strategic plan (introduced 2015)
- The **Georgia School Climate Survey** is administered to assess and track school climate outcomes

*Source: [Georgia Department of Education — School Climate](https://gadoe.org/whole-child-supports/school-climate/)*

### State-Level SEL Implementation

- Georgia has provided grants to community organizations that deliver evidence-based support for students' learning, connectedness, and well-being
- The state recognizes that SEL occurs across school and community settings

*Source: [CASEL SEL Policy at the State Level](http://casel.org/systemic-implementation/sel-policy-at-the-state-level/)*

## SEL in Out-of-School-Time (OST) Programs and Community Youth Organizations

### RAND/Wallace Foundation Research on Afterschool SEL

**Study Overview:**
The **Partnerships for Social and Emotional Learning Initiative (PSELI)**, a four-year longitudinal study by the RAND Corporation and The Wallace Foundation, examined SEL implementation in:
- **More than 100 afterschool programs** across **six communities**
- Both school-based and out-of-school time settings working in partnership

**Key Findings on OST Programs:**

1. **Positive Outcomes in Afterschool Settings:**
   - Program participants showed improvements in feelings and attitudes
   - Behavioral adjustment gains
   - Academic performance improvements

2. **Advantage of OST Settings:**
   - OST programs have "more flexibility and are less structured than traditional schooling," creating opportunities for SEL skill-building through varied activities
   - Evidence supports the positive impact of foundational quality (staff training, program structure) on youth outcomes
   - **Enhanced focus on SEL in OST settings produced additional positive gains**, even during challenging periods (e.g., COVID-19 pandemic)

3. **Skills with Strongest Evidence Base in Youth Programs:**
   - **Communication** – expressing ideas clearly, listening actively
   - **Collaboration** – teamwork, group problem-solving
   - **Conflict resolution** – managing disagreements constructively
   - **Leadership** – taking responsibility, influencing peers positively

*Sources: [RAND — Skills for Success](https://www.rand.org/pubs/research_reports/RRA379-11.html); [Wallace Foundation — Early Lessons from Schools and OST Programs](https://wallacefoundation.org/report/early-lessons-schools-and-ost-programs)*

### The SAFE Criteria for Effective OST SEL Programs

Research-based guidance for delivering SEL in out-of-school-time programs emphasizes four core design principles, known as the **SAFE approach**:

1. **Sequenced** – Skills instruction is planned as a series of connected and coordinated steps so that social and emotional skills build upon each other and strengthen over time; skills are taught in developmental progression.

2. **Active** – Instruction uses active forms of learning (experiential activities, project-based learning, real-life application, discussion, problem-solving scenarios) rather than lecture-based teaching.

3. **Focused** – Time and attention are deliberately devoted to developing personal or social skills; SEL is intentional rather than incidental to other activities.

4. **Explicit** – Those skills are named, defined, and directly taught; youth understand what skill is being practiced and why it matters.

**Application to Youth Organizations:**
Programs like Cub Scouts, Boy Scouts, Girl Scouts, Boys & Girls Clubs, 4-H, and other youth development organizations can align their existing activities (outdoor adventures, skill-building projects, service learning) with SAFE principles to intentionally build SEL competencies.

*Sources: [CASEL Schoolguide — Explicit SEL Instruction](https://schoolguide.casel.org/focus-area-3/classroom/explicit-sel-instruction/); [Wallace Foundation — SEL in Out-of-School Time Settings](https://wallacefoundation.org/sites/default/files/2023-09/Social-and-Emotional-Learning-Out-of-School-Time-Settings-Brief.pdf)*

### Distinction: School-Based vs. OST SEL Findings

**School-Based SEL Research (Durlak 2011, Taylor 2017):**
- Examined curricula-based, universal programs delivered during the school day
- Effect sizes for academic achievement: 0.27–0.33 (11 percentile-point gain)
- Outcomes rigorously measured across large samples with controlled comparisons

**Out-of-School-Time SEL Research (RAND/Wallace):**
- Examined programs delivered in afterschool, summer, and community settings
- Outcomes include behavioral adjustment, attitudes, academic performance, and life skills (communication, collaboration, leadership)
- Programs benefit from flexibility in design and greater opportunity for youth-centered, experiential learning
- Qualitative and implementation research emphasizes the importance of quality and explicit SEL integration

**Important Note:**
While school-based curricula have larger, more precisely measured effect sizes (due to standardized measurement and control designs), research supports that OST programs with intentional SEL integration produce meaningful, measurable improvements in youth outcomes. The mechanisms differ (curriculum-based instruction vs. experiential skill practice), but both contexts contribute to systemic SEL development.

## Research Summary: Applicability to Cub Scout Programs

### Evidence Supporting Community-Based SEL Programs

1. **CASEL Framework recognizes communities as essential partners:**
   - SEL happens in classrooms, schools, homes, **and communities**
   - Community organizations have a key role in extending SEL experiences

2. **Research documents OST program effectiveness:**
   - Afterschool and youth development programs can deliver SEL with positive outcomes
   - SAFE criteria provide evidence-based design principles for youth organizations
   - Benefits include improved attitudes, behavior, and academic performance

3. **Economic case is strong:**
   - 11:1 benefit-cost ratio for SEL interventions (Belfield et al. 2015)
   - Lifetime earnings gains from improved SEL outcomes
   - More cost-effective than many academic interventions

4. **Georgia alignment:**
   - State has adopted CASEL-aligned SEL framework
   - Early learning standards embed SEL
   - School climate is a state priority
   - Georgia recognizes SEL occurs across school and community settings

### Key Message for School Districts

School districts seeking to support SEL can partner with community youth organizations (such as Cub Scouts) to:
- **Extend SEL practice** beyond the school day into community settings where children spend significant time
- **Align activities** with the five CASEL competencies and use SAFE principles (Sequenced, Active, Focused, Explicit)
- **Leverage existing youth program strengths** (mentoring, peer interaction, real-world skill practice, community service) as natural contexts for SEL
- **Reduce burden on schools** by distributing SEL support across multiple settings and trusted community partners
- **Support equitable outcomes** by ensuring all children access SEL across multiple contexts
